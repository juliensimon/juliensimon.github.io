Ticking Boxes, Shipping Models

Author: Julien Simon

Date: October 15, 2026 · 34 min read

Source: https://www.airealist.ai/p/ticking-boxes-shipping-models

Listen: AI-narrated audio of this piece, 21 min. From The AI Realist podcast, also on Apple Podcasts and Spotify.

Doctolib’s help center tells patients their conversations with its AI assistant “are encrypted end to end and hosted in France according to the strictest European standards (GDPR, HDS certification)”. Its July privacy policy says the stored data sits with Amazon Web Services and S3NS, a Thales company reselling Google Cloud, and that Microsoft (Azure), Google, and Anthropic, three US companies, supply the model behind the assistant on servers the policy places in the EU.[1][2] Doctolib says its patient assistant is “already used by 400,000 French people”.[3] On October 13th, 2026, it announced a planned minority investment from Nvidia and a plan to build “sovereign” models on open-weight and open-source models.[3][4]

None of this is a scandal. It maps what a French health platform can buy, and what it must build itself once the regulator’s boxes are ticked. Every box costs engineering time that doesn't go into the product. Doctolib does most of the hard engineering right. It slips in the words: one sentence to patients, one sentence in a press release. No published sanction or ruling finds Doctolib in breach of health-data or data-protection rules, and in 2021 a Conseil d’État judge refused to suspend its Covid vaccination bookings over its use of AWS.[5] Where those rules are unclear, they are unclear for everyone.

The hoops

Beyond informing patients, French law requires a health platform to have a host certified for health data (HDS), store data within the European Economic Area (a rule since September 26th, 2026), and sign a contract that lists the foreign laws that can reach the data.[6] It does not ask a private company for SecNumCloud, France’s cloud-security qualification, or for a host immune to US law. Those duties bind the State and the Health Data Hub for their most sensitive data.[6]

The boxes also move. The 2018 standard set no location rule, only disclosure of the countries and the client’s choice among them; the EEA rule arrived in the 2024 standard, then in law, in a version that calls its new transfer rules “a first step” and promises stronger European-sovereignty requirements “by 2027 at the latest”, with nothing yet saying what they will be. The data regulator, the CNIL, already recommends SecNumCloud “for the most sensitive databases”, while calling the risk “often acceptable” for countries with an adequacy decision, which the US has for companies certified under the EU-US Data Privacy Framework.[7] A platform built for today’s rules may need rebuilding for 2027’s. Spare a thought for the French and European teams building to this: certificates, transfer maps, and contract annexes, written for a target that keeps moving. The work still has to be done, and the people doing it have earned some patience.

What Doctolib gets right

Most of the boxes, ticked properly. Doctolib is certified in its own name for all six HDS activities, including the one AWS’s first certificate left to its customers. Its encryption keys sit with Atos in France, away from the hosts: in 2020 its chief executive, Stanislas Niox-Chateau, said: “there is no way for AWS to access the data”.[2][8] And it says the uncomfortable thing in plain words: AWS’s parent being American, “in specific cases, AWS may therefore be required to comply with the law or with a valid and binding order”, in which case “personal data may be accessed from outside the EEA, in particular from the United States”.[2] Compliance by confession, and legal. Using the assistant requires prior consent to the processing of health data under GDPR Article 9.[1]

The gap underneath

Why AWS at all? Because European clouds aren't the same product.I called it the Builder Tax in April: about 60 services at Scaleway, Iliad’s cloud, against more than 200 at each hyperscaler, as counted then. The missing ones get rebuilt in engineering time.[9]

Doctolib and four other French health-tech companies put a price on it. In a letter to the office of the minister for digital affairs, reported by L’Usine Digitale in July, they asked the government to state officially that SecNumCloud is not a general obligation for health establishments and their suppliers. Per the outlet, some managed services that health applications depend on do not exist on the qualified platforms, “the current SecNumCloud-qualified offers are not ready yet”, and a move could cost “20 to 50%” more and tie engineering teams up for several years on infrastructure instead of products.[10] A lobbying letter, and a fair one. The minister’s office answered, per the outlet, that SecNumCloud will not become a general obligation for health companies.[10]

If the State can get away with six years, why would you ask a scale-up to do it in one?

The State ran the same test on itself. Its Health Data Hub has run on Microsoft Azure from the start. Under oath before the Senate in June 2025, Microsoft France’s director of public and legal affairs was asked whether French citizens’ data entrusted to Microsoft through UGAP, the public purchasing agency, could be handed to US authorities without France’s consent: “No, I cannot guarantee it, but, again, it has never happened yet.”[11] Almost six years after the first court challenge, in June 2020, the State picked Scaleway to take over the Hub, in April 2026, a cloud not yet SecNumCloud-qualified.[11] If the State can get away with six years, why would you ask a scale-up to do it in one?

The gap is wider for models

Storage has European options with fewer features. For closed models, sold only as a service, every supplier Doctolib names is American, and their EU routes run through US clouds.[2][12][13] Open-weight models are different: whoever holds the weights decides where they run.

Anthropic is not on the French list of certified health-data hosts; Microsoft and Google are.[14] It is also not on the US list of companies certified under the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, where Microsoft, Google, and Amazon Web Services are; its data-processing terms for direct customers rely on the Commission’s standard contractual clauses instead.[15] Anthropic’s own API offers two settings for where a request is processed:global, the default, where “Inference may run in any available geography”, andus.[12] For Europe, it points customers to AWS, Google, and Microsoft, and on AWS’s Bedrock a request from Paris for Claude Opus 5.5 can be routed to any of six EU regions, with no Paris-only option. Bedrock can pin some older Claude models to a single EU region, Stockholm or Ireland, but none to Paris or Frankfurt; outside the EU, Opus 5.5 pins to London.[16] In Doctolib’s practitioner contract, “Google Ireland (Gemini)” lists servers in the “EU – United States”, the only model-supplier row in either Doctolib document whose server location includes the United States.[13] A Doctolib spokesperson told Next those rows cover features “such as the transcription and summary of medical consultations”.[17] The patient policy’s Azure row may not even mean an American model: Azure also serves models from Mistral AI, a French company, and Microsoft’s 2024 account put Doctolib’s consultation assistant on Azure OpenAI and on Mistral Large.[18]

Retention is the soft spot behind the denial. In June, Le Canard enchaîné wrote, per Next, in a story about practitioners’ consultation notes, that Doctolib was handing “its users’ information to American AI giants” to train their models. Doctolib denied it: “a strict contractual framework forbids them to retain or exploit the data for their own account”. The CNIL told the Canard, per Solutions Numériques, it could not rule on the legality.[17][19] Anthropic’s commercial terms back the training half: it “may not train models on Customer Content”.[20] Retention is the half that depends on the route, and the route is not published. Anthropic’s own API stores data in the US and deletes inputs and outputs “within 30 days” by default, unless agreed otherwise, for instance under a zero-retention agreement.

On Bedrock, AWS says model providers “don’t have access to Amazon Bedrock logs or to customer prompts and completions”. It keeps prompts and responses, “up to 30 days”, only for Claude Fable 5 and 5.1, where retention is required, and an account can enforce zero retention, under which nothing is “written to durable storage by AWS or shared with the model provider”; for Claude models, Anthropic decides who is eligible. Doctolib lists Anthropic’s servers in the EU, which Anthropic’s own API does not offer, so a cloud route is likely. Which cloud is not published, and the published contract with practitioners promises only “equivalent” protection from sub-processors.[13][20] Run through Bedrock’s EU profile with zero retention, the setup is defensible; nothing Doctolib publishes says that is how it runs.

Whether health-data law reaches a model supplier’s API remains unsettled. French law requires a certificate of anyone who digitally “hosts” health data collected in care on someone else’s behalf, and defines hosting by activities, including running infrastructure “used to process health data”; an exclusion for operations of short duration “covers only” data entry, formatting, and conversion to and from paper, per the health agency (ANS). The same agency says a certified host’s subcontractors need not be certified themselves, and Doctolib is certified for all six activities.[21] I found no ruling or guidance that says whether a model that reads a prompt and answers it is hosting. Doctolib’s February transfer map lists AWS and none of the three model suppliers.[22]

The AI Act reaches the assistant, on a delay. Doctolib is its provider in the Act’s sense; the developers of the general-purpose AI models underneath have had their own duties since August 2025. Since August 2026, people must be told they are talking to an AI; a feature called “Assistant IA” tells them in its name. The open question is classification. The Act lists “emergency healthcare patient triage systems” as high-risk. Doctolib’s terms say the assistant offers general guidance on “emergencies, medical consultation”, then say it “is not a medical device” and must not be used “to manage a medical emergency”. If a regulator reads that guidance as triage anyway, the high-risk duties apply from December 2nd, 2027, after the Digital Omnibus pushed them back from August 2026. None of it decides which company’s model reads the prompt, or where.[23]

Where it slips into theater

It cannot describe the step where a model supplied by a US company, on servers the policy places in the EU, reads a parent’s question about a sick child.

The help-center sentence is the mistake, and it is not a small one. Encryption in transit and at rest protects data on the wire and on disk; to answer, a model must process the text decrypted. Clubic raised it in June.[24] “Encrypted end to end and hosted in France” can describe the trip to Doctolib and the storage there. It cannot describe the step where a model supplied by a US company, on servers the policy places in the EU, reads a parent’s question about a sick child. The help center says nothing of that step, and the consent text Doctolib publishes does not name the model suppliers; the names sit in a policy annex. They arrived late. A help page last edited in December 2025 already described the assistant as in test, using “the information entered in your child’s Health profile”, while the privacy policy in force until April 2026 named no model supplier and did not mention the assistant.[1][2] Even “in France” is generous. The first page of Doctolib’s own July patient policy says patients’ personal data, health data included, are “hosted within the European Union”; its annex lists AWS’s servers in the “European Union”, and its practitioner contract puts the “primary cluster” in AWS’s Frankfurt region, a secondary one in Paris and a third system with S3NS in the Netherlands.[2][13] That help-center page is what patients read before they type, and it describes the easy part.

The press release does the same for everyone else. “Doctolib hosts all health data in the European Union and the UK [...] under its sole control. The same applies to its AI models.”[4] Read generously, “its AI models” means the ones Doctolib builds, since the sentence follows “Doctolib is developing its own AI models”. Its health data sits with AWS and S3NS, under a US-law exposure Doctolib itself discloses, and the July policy lists three US companies as suppliers of the patient assistant’s model.[2][4] “Sole control” is a claim about today, and today’s documents do not back it.

Doctolib supplies the word, Nvidia the chip

Running open-weight models itself closes the model gap, and Doctolib’s post to users says how: “We run our copies in our secure environments in Europe and train them on medical knowledge, without transmitting health data”, while a closed model “can only be used through the service of the company that owns it”.[3] That is the right fix, and a candid description of the setup it replaces. Doctolib already has the parts to build it. It is already a certified health-data host, its consultation assistant runs on a speech-recognition model “developed by its teams” (whether built from scratch or fine-tuned from an open model, and under what license, is not published), and its lab publishes open-weight French medical text encoders and classifiers, not chat models; one, DoctoModernBERT, is under Apache-2.0 and was trained on Jean Zay, a French public supercomputer run by IDRIS.[4][8][25] Nothing stops it from running a model under its control throughout: open weights, its own fine-tuning, on a certified host with no US parent if it picks one.

The three US suppliers are a choice. It may be a sound one, but the help center should say so. The plan is also partial by design: the same post says open-weight and open-source models mean Doctolib will “not depend exclusively” on the big general-purpose models.[3] The post does give one date: the assistant will be available to everyone in 2027.[3] The announcement does not give a date for the patient assistant to leave Azure, Google, and Anthropic, or the name of the cloud its own models will run on.[3][4]

The exit has the same shape as the gap. Running open-weight models yourself means renting or buying AI chips, and European clouds rent out chips Europe does not make: Scaleway’s GPU instance page lists only Nvidia chips, though it announced an AMD supercomputer in Sweden the same day as Doctolib’s news.[26] Doctolib “has been using NVIDIA technologies for a year”, per its release, and Jensen Huang told analysts in August: “I would say nearly all open models run on NVIDIA.”[4][27]

A builder that swaps a closed model’s API for an open-weight model it runs itself trades a US company processing its prompts for a US company selling it chips.

That is why a stake of a few million euros, per Le Figaro as reported by Next, is worth Nvidia’s time; Doctolib says it comes “without governance rights or a board seat”.[3][4] In the six months to July 26th, 2026, Nvidia paid $42.4 billion for equity securities (gross), what its 10-Q calls “investing in our ecosystem”, more than the $39.0 billion it paid for share buybacks.[28] Sovereign AI, neoclouds, start-ups and enterprises, Huang said, “represents about half of our business, and that’s growing 100% a year.”[29] A builder that swaps a closed model’s API for an open-weight model it runs itself trades a US company processing its prompts for a US company selling it chips. Doctolib calls the models “sovereign”. Nvidia is paid either way.

Four things would change this verdict. Doctolib publishes EU-only processing and zero-retention terms for all three suppliers. Or it shows that text is pseudonymized before it reaches them. Or the patient assistant already runs on models Doctolib runs itself, and the policy rows are stale. Or the models it builds on open weights run on Amazon’s Trainium, Google’s TPUs, or AMD GPUs, and Nvidia invested in a customer it didn’t keep.

Until then, anyone building or buying a health AI product in Europe needs four answers: where the data is stored, who can compel the host, which company’s model reads the prompt, and how long the prompt is kept. Doctolib answers the first two publicly, the third by naming three suppliers in an annex, and the fourth through a spokesperson. Answers that hold up live in privacy policies, contract annexes, and system configurations, not in speeches, press releases, social media posts, or a spokesperson’s word.

On sovereignty, the gap between what gets announced and what gets deployed can be wide, and there is no substitute for deep engineering work to close it. Nvidia only cares about a fifth question: what does it run on? Jensen’s cash is a bet that the answer is Nvidia.
